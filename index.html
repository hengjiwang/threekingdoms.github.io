The story so far

The Great River rolls east, carrying away every trace of proud and gallant heroes. Right and wrong, glory and failure, all become fleeting illusions, while the green hills remain beneath the fading western light.

Fishermen and woodcutters gather along the river islands. Grey-haired, they have watched many springs and autumns pass. Over a jar of wine, the famous deeds of earlier ages become stories for good company.

-- Yang Shen (1488-1559), translated by Moss Roberts.

At the end of the Eastern Han dynasty, the foolishness of the Emperor and cruel oppression by landlords made the lives of peasants miserable. Finally, in 184 AD, a peasant revolt led by Zhang Jue broke out and swept across China in a short time. The weak Han central government had to encourage local officers to build their own armed forces to suppress the rebels, which facilitated the formation of regional warlords.

Meanwhile, in the imperial palace, the court eunuchs gradually became influential and started abusing their power. To exterminate the eunuchs, Marshal He Jin invited the Hedong warlord Dong Zhuo to the capital, Luoyang. However, the eunuchs preemptively assassinated He Jin and kidnapped the Emperor. Immediately afterward, the prestigious noble Yuan Shao led an army into the palace and killed all the court eunuchs, thus suppressing the chaos. At the same time, Dong Zhuo and his army were reaching the capital.

After Dong Zhuo arrived at court, he rapidly grasped supreme power and behaved arrogantly and overbearingly. In September 189 AD, Dong Zhuo dethroned the current Emperor and chose Liu Xie, an eight-year-old child, as the new Emperor. This action greatly angered the warlords and nobles. Led by Yuan Shao, the warlords formed a coalition against Dong Zhuo and marched on Luoyang. To evade the crusade, Dong Zhuo burned Luoyang and moved the capital to Chang'an. Dong Zhuo’s atrocities made him deeply unpopular. Under a scheme by Wang Yun, the relationship between Dong Zhuo and his adopted son Lu Bu deteriorated. Finally, in 192 AD, Lu Bu slew Dong Zhuo.

After Dong Zhuo died, his former generals Li Jue and Guo Si marched into Chang'an, killed Wang Yun, and expelled the young Emperor. Meanwhile, regional warlords held wild ambitions and started attacking and annexing one another. No one still cared about the young Emperor except Cao Cao, who realized the enormous value behind the title. In 196 AD, Cao Cao invited the Emperor to his base at Xuchang, immediately took control of him, and began commanding and attacking other warlords in the name of the Emperor’s edict. With this legitimacy and his exceptional leadership, Cao Cao conquered Lu Bu, Zhang Xiu, Zhang Yang, and others, and annexed the whole Central Plain around 199 AD. Cao Cao’s rapid rise shocked Yuan Shao. Yuan Shao demanded that Cao Cao hand over the young Emperor, but Cao Cao refused. A final battle between them became inevitable.

In February 200 AD, Yuan Shao led 110,000 soldiers marching on Guandu, aiming to cross the Yellow River and annihilate Cao Cao. Cao Cao led 40,000 soldiers and resisted fiercely, killing Yuan Shao’s top generals Yan Liang and Wen Chou. The battles lasted for eight months, until Cao Cao exhausted his supplies and fell into danger. In October 200 AD, Cao Cao adopted the suggestion of Xu You and led 5,000 soldiers in a surprise attack on Wuchao. He burned all of Yuan Shao’s supplies, then immediately assaulted Yuan Shao’s base and destroyed his main force. Cao Cao spent seven years eliminating Yuan’s remnants. In 207 AD, Cao Cao unified northern China.

In 208 AD, Cao Cao appointed himself Prime Minister of the Han dynasty and began his plan to unify China. In August 208 AD, Jingzhou’s warlord Liu Biao died. Cao Cao then led 190,000 soldiers toward Jingzhou and subdued Liu Biao’s heir Liu Cong. Liu Biao's subordinate Liu Bei escaped to Wu, seeking a coalition with Sun Quan against Cao Cao.

Meanwhile, Cao Cao led 260,000 soldiers marching on Wu. Sun Quan ordered his marshal Zhou Yu to lead 30,000 troops, together with Liu Bei’s 20,000 soldiers, to resist Cao Cao. The two sides confronted each other at Red Cliffs, divided by the Yangtze River. In November 208 AD, Zhou Yu used a false-surrender scheme and rushed ten burning warships into Cao Cao’s base, setting fire to all of Cao Cao’s warships and camps. Zhou Yu and Liu Bei then hunted down Cao Cao’s retreating army. Cao Cao lost 100,000 soldiers, and his dream of unifying China was broken.

After the Battle of Red Cliffs, Liu Bei rapidly conquered Jingzhou, then conquered Yizhou in 214 AD. In 219 AD, to contest legitimacy with Cao Cao, who had claimed to be King of Wei, Liu Bei conquered Hanzhong and claimed to be King of Hanzhong. Meanwhile, Liu Bei’s commander Guan Yu conquered Xiangyang and besieged Fancheng. Liu Bei’s career reached its peak.

However, in October 219 AD, Sun Quan broke the coalition and launched a surprise attack on Jingzhou, capturing and executing Guan Yu. In 220 AD, Cao Cao died, and his heir Cao Pi usurped the Han dynasty and claimed to be Emperor of Wei. In response, Liu Bei claimed to be Emperor of the Han dynasty (Shu Han), then led 40,000 soldiers marching on Wu in the name of revenge for Guan Yu. In 222 AD, Liu Bei was defeated by Lu Xun at Yiling, retreated to Baidicheng, and fell seriously ill. He entrusted his heir Liu Shan to Prime Minister Zhuge Liang, then died in 223 AD.

After Liu Bei died, Shu Han was at the edge of collapse, and the new Emperor Liu Shan was commonly regarded as mediocre. Thus, Zhuge Liang began taking charge of almost all governing affairs and was regarded as a savior by all. Zhuge Liang rebuilt the coalition with Wu, subdued the southern barbarians, recovered the economy, promoted talented officials and generals, restored the military power, and repelled Wei’s invasions. Then, from 228 to 234 AD, Zhuge Liang organized five Northern Expeditions against Wei in the name of restoring the Han dynasty. Facing Wei, which was much stronger than Shu Han, Zhuge Liang held a military advantage and defeated Wei’s top generals many times. His rival Sima Yi praised him as “an extraordinary genius under Heaven.” Unfortunately, exhausted by a tremendous number of affairs involving the economy, military, and personnel, Zhuge Liang died in the army in 234 AD, at the age of 54.

After Zhuge Liang died, his disciple Jiang Wei inherited the military leadership of Shu Han and organized eleven Northern Expeditions against Wei, but gained little. In 263 AD, Wei’s generals Deng Ai and Zhong Hui led 180,000 soldiers marching on Chengdu. Emperor Liu Shan surrendered, and Shu Han fell. The long period of confrontation against Shu Han gave the Sima family supreme power in Wei. Finally, in 266 AD, Sima Yan usurped the Wei dynasty and claimed to be Emperor of Jin. In 279 AD, Jin conquered Wu and unified China, which marked the end of the Three Kingdoms period.
