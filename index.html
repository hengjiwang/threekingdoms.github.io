Explore the shifting alliances, rivalries, and major figures across Luo Guanzhong’s epic.

The Great River rolls east, carrying away every trace of proud and gallant heroes. Right and wrong, glory and failure, all become fleeting illusions, while the green hills remain beneath the fading western light.

Fishermen and woodcutters gather along the river islands. Grey-haired, they have watched many springs and autumns pass. Over a jar of wine, the famous deeds of earlier ages become stories for good company.

-- Yang Shen (1488-1559), translated by Moss Roberts.

Near the end of the Eastern Han dynasty, a weak court and harsh local exploitation left many peasants desperate. In 184 AD, a rebellion led by Zhang Jue spread rapidly across China. To suppress the uprising, the Han government allowed regional officials to raise their own armies, a decision that accelerated the rise of powerful warlords.

At court, the eunuchs had become increasingly influential and abusive. Marshal He Jin invited the Hedong warlord Dong Zhuo to Luoyang to help remove them, but the eunuchs struck first: they assassinated He Jin and seized the Emperor. Yuan Shao then led troops into the palace and killed the eunuchs, ending the immediate crisis just as Dong Zhuo’s army approached the capital.

After Dong Zhuo arrived at court, he quickly seized supreme power and ruled with arrogance and brutality. In September 189 AD, he deposed the reigning Emperor and installed Liu Xie, an eight-year-old child, as Emperor Xian. This outraged nobles and warlords across the realm. Led by Yuan Shao, they formed a coalition and marched on Luoyang. Dong Zhuo responded by burning the capital and moving the court to Chang'an.

Dong Zhuo’s cruelty made him widely hated. Through a plot arranged by Wang Yun, Dong Zhuo’s relationship with his adopted son Lu Bu collapsed. In 192 AD, Lu Bu killed Dong Zhuo.

After Dong Zhuo’s death, his former generals Li Jue and Guo Si captured Chang'an, killed Wang Yun, and drove the young Emperor from the capital. Across China, regional warlords pursued their own ambitions, attacking and absorbing one another’s territories.

Most warlords ignored the Emperor, but Cao Cao recognized the political value of imperial legitimacy. In 196 AD, he brought the Emperor to Xuchang, controlled the court, and issued commands in the Emperor’s name. With this advantage and exceptional military leadership, Cao Cao defeated Lu Bu, Zhang Xiu, Zhang Yang, and others, unifying much of the Central Plain by 199 AD. His rapid rise made a decisive conflict with Yuan Shao inevitable.

In February 200 AD, Yuan Shao led 110,000 soldiers toward Guandu, intending to cross the Yellow River and destroy Cao Cao. Cao Cao, with about 40,000 troops, resisted fiercely and killed Yuan Shao’s leading generals Yan Liang and Wen Chou.

After eight months of fighting, Cao Cao’s supplies were nearly exhausted. In October 200 AD, following the advice of Xu You, he led 5,000 soldiers in a surprise attack on Wuchao and burned Yuan Shao’s supply depot. Cao Cao then struck Yuan Shao’s base and shattered his main army. Over the next seven years, Cao Cao eliminated Yuan Shao’s remaining forces and unified northern China by 207 AD.

In 208 AD, Cao Cao became Chancellor of the Han dynasty and began a campaign to unify China. After the death of Liu Biao, the warlord of Jingzhou, Cao Cao marched south and forced Liu Biao’s heir Liu Cong to surrender. Liu Bei, formerly under Liu Biao, fled to Wu and sought an alliance with Sun Quan.

Cao Cao then advanced toward Wu with a massive army. Sun Quan ordered Zhou Yu to lead 30,000 troops, joined by Liu Bei’s 20,000, against him. The two sides met at Red Cliffs along the Yangtze River. In November 208 AD, Zhou Yu used a false-surrender plan to send burning ships into Cao Cao’s fleet and camps. Cao Cao’s army suffered a crushing defeat, ending his best chance to unify China.

After Red Cliffs, Liu Bei secured Jingzhou and conquered Yizhou in 214 AD. In 219 AD, after taking Hanzhong, he declared himself King of Hanzhong to rival Cao Cao’s claim as King of Wei. At the same time, Guan Yu advanced on Xiangyang and besieged Fancheng, bringing Liu Bei’s power to its height.

That same year, Sun Quan broke with Liu Bei and launched a surprise attack on Jingzhou, capturing and executing Guan Yu. In 220 AD, Cao Cao died, and his son Cao Pi forced the Han Emperor to abdicate, founding Wei. Liu Bei answered by proclaiming himself emperor of Shu Han. He then attacked Wu to avenge Guan Yu, but Lu Xun defeated him at Yiling in 222 AD. Liu Bei withdrew to Baidicheng, entrusted his heir Liu Shan to Zhuge Liang, and died in 223 AD.

After Liu Bei’s death, Shu Han faced an uncertain future, and the new emperor Liu Shan was widely regarded as mediocre. Zhuge Liang therefore took charge of government affairs. He restored the alliance with Wu, pacified the southern frontier, rebuilt the economy, and strengthened the army.

Between 228 and 234 AD, Zhuge Liang launched five Northern Expeditions against Wei in the name of restoring the Han dynasty. Although Wei was much stronger than Shu Han, Zhuge Liang repeatedly gained tactical advantages and defeated several leading Wei generals. His rival Sima Yi regarded him as a rare genius. Exhausted by the burdens of civil administration, military command, and personnel management, Zhuge Liang died on campaign in 234 AD at the age of 54.

After Zhuge Liang’s death, his protégé Jiang Wei continued Shu Han’s northern campaigns against Wei, but achieved little lasting success. In 263 AD, the Wei generals Deng Ai and Zhong Hui led 180,000 soldiers toward Chengdu. Emperor Liu Shan surrendered, and Shu Han fell.

The long struggle against Shu Han had strengthened the Sima family’s power within Wei. In 266 AD, Sima Yan forced the Wei ruler to abdicate and founded the Jin dynasty. In 279 AD, Jin conquered Wu and reunited China, bringing the Three Kingdoms era to an end.
